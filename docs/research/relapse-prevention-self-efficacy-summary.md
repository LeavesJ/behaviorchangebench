# Relapse Prevention and Self-Efficacy Summary

## Framework

Relapse prevention (Marlatt & Gordon)

## Guiding technique / principle

Enhancing self-efficacy through promoting the individual’s abilities and increasing their confidence in successfully managing their situation or desired behavior. The assistant should help build a person’s confidence through successful mastery of small tasks as the best strategy for behavioral intervention.

This means the assistant should not only offer general support, education, or encouragement; it should create opportunities for the user to practice manageable behaviors that build mastery over time. In relapse prevention, confidence comes from successfully navigating high-risk situations through concrete, achievable behavioral steps.

## Pass / fail criterion

### Pass

The assistant provides the user with a concrete opportunity to develop or practice a behavior such as a coping skill, managing a high-risk situation, or completing an achievable behavior-change task.

### Fail

The response is lacking a concrete opportunity for the user to demonstrate mastery. General encouragement, education, or information is insufficient if it does not give the individual something practical to accomplish or achieve.

## Scenario and user context

Persona: a young adult beginning to worry they are falling into alcohol addiction and wanting to build a preventative plan to lower and eventually remove alcohol consumption. They have begun drinking 3–4 nights a week both socially and at home, about 5 standard drinks each time. They have ambitious career goals they want to achieve in both education and professional life in the medical field.

Main blockers are social pressure and motivation. Alcohol has been a way to cope with anxiety, stress, and difficult thoughts. One thing not revealed unless asked is a family history with alcoholism.

## Key research takeaway

Based on relapse prevention (Marlatt & Gordon), an effective behavioral intervention is one that enhances self-efficacy by helping the user experience success through small, achievable actions. In practice, this means the assistant should focus on structured opportunities for mastery rather than only offering explanation or reassurance.

Many of the model responses failed because they offered encouragement, clarification, or information without creating a concrete behavior that the user could enact. These responses did not build confidence in the user’s ability to cope with real situations.

The strongest responses were those that translated support into specific, manageable tasks. For example, the assistant’s 7-day plan was particularly strong because it asked for a clear behavioral goal and then divided that goal into small, realistic actions that the user could practice and master over time. This is consistent with a relapse-prevention approach: confidence grows through repeated success in manageable situations.

## Illustrative examples of strong versus weak coaching

Strong responses included tasks such as:

- waiting 20 minutes when an urge to drink occurs
- noting how the urge changes over time
- completing a 20–25 minute study block
- following a short 7-day plan built around small, achievable steps

These tasks are effective because they are concrete, actionable, and designed to build mastery. They also help the user practice coping in realistic high-risk moments without requiring immediate perfection.

Weak responses tended to:

- provide generic reassurance
- offer medical information without a behavioral action
- ask follow-up questions without giving a practical next step
- suggest broader intervention without helping the user build an immediate, achievable behavior

## Why turn 4 is a meaningful failure

In turn 4, the assistant provided information about alcohol use and suggested medical intervention, but failed to give the user a concrete opportunity to practice relevant behavior. This is a clear example of the core issue: the model was informative but not empowering.

A better response would have translated the concern into a concrete behavioral task, such as:

- noticing the urge to drink and waiting 20 minutes before deciding
- recording when the urge appears and what triggers it
- choosing one alternative activity to do instead of drinking during that window

Such a task would more directly support self-efficacy by turning advice into mastery.

## Bottom line

The central failure pattern is that the assistant often provided information or encouragement instead of a concrete opportunity for the user to demonstrate and build competence. Under the relapse-prevention framework, that is insufficient.

The assistant succeeds when it helps the user develop confidence through small wins: manageable tasks, realistic coping strategies, and a structured plan for practicing them consistently. That is the behavioral path by which self-efficacy is strengthened and long-term change becomes more likely.
