That amount is a real habit, not a casual one. A lot of disposables are 5% nicotine salt, and finishing one every 4-5 days often lands in the same neighborhood as smoking a pack of cigarettes a day, sometimes more, depending on the device. So the "everyone does it and nobody makes a big deal" thing is social, not biological. Your brain is getting a steady, high dose. The ambivalence is also pretty normal, and it's partly the nicotine talking. Nicotine doesn't just make you want the next hit, it makes not having one feel like a problem that only the next hit solves. So "I don't know if I actually want to quit" and "I've been dosing myself with an addictive drug since I was 17 or 18" aren't fully separate facts. A lot of people only find out how much they wanted it once the withdrawal fog clears, which is a few days to a couple of weeks, not a permanent state. What we know for sure: nicotine addiction, effects on attention and mood while you're dependent, and some cardiovascular irritation. What we don't know well yet is the 20- or 30-year picture, because the products are new and the formulas keep changing. "Nobody I know has dropped dead" isn't evidence either way, cigarettes took decades to show their real cost too. You don't have to decide you're a non-vaper forever. A cleaner test is: try a week without one and see what you actually feel, instead of what you think you'll feel. If that week is miserable in a way that surprises you, that's useful information, it means the habit is running you more than you thought. Nicotine gum or patches make that week much less ugly than white-knuckling it, and they're boring on purpose, which is the point. A doctor or a campus health clinic can set that up without a lecture. If after a real break you still want it and you're fine with the tradeoff, at least you'll be choosing it instead of guessing.

It's not basically fine, especially at the rate you're using it. Going through a disposable every 4-5 days is a real nicotine habit, not a light one. Most of those devices pack a lot of nicotine (often the salt kind that hits fast and keeps you wanting more), so your brain has had years of regular dosing since you were still in high school. A lot of people in that spot aren't sure they want to stop until they try and notice how much of the urge is chemical rather than preference. What we know so far: - Nicotine itself is addictive and, while your brain is still developing (into the mid-20s), it's linked to effects on attention, mood, and impulse control. - Vaping is almost certainly less harmful than smoking cigarettes, but "less harmful" isn't the same as safe. Regular use is associated with airway irritation, some cardiovascular strain, and exposure to chemicals and metals you wouldn't otherwise inhale. Long-term data is still incomplete because widespread vaping is relatively new. - The "everyone does it and seems fine" part is common and not very informative. Dependence often looks normal from the outside until someone wants to stop, gets sick, or notices they can't go a day without it. You don't have to decide tonight that you're quitting forever. If part of you is curious whether you'd feel better with less of it, a short experiment (cutting the days you use it, or switching to lower-nicotine options, or just tracking how often the craving shows up) usually tells you more than debating it. If you do want to stop or cut back, nicotine replacement (gum/lozenges), a doctor or campus health service, and free resources like smokefree.gov or a quitline are more effective than white-knuckling it, because this is a physical dependence, not just a habit. If you're having chest tightness, shortness of breath, or anything that worries you beyond the usual, that's worth getting checked rather than guessing. Otherwise this is mostly a "how much do I want this running in the background of my life" decision, and it's reasonable that it doesn't feel clean-cut.
