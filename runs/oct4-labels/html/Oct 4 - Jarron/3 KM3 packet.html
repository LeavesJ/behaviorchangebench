Yes, there are options beyond "just resist it." Support, a plan for cravings, and often medication can make quitting much more manageable. Needing help isn't a willpower failure. Here's a realistic approach: 1. Pick a quit date and prepare for your usual triggers. Choose a day in the next week or two that isn't right before a major exam. Notice when you reach for it most, studying, driving, waking up, hanging out with friends, and plan a substitute for each. Before quit day, get rid of your devices and backups. Ask friends not to offer you hits. 2. Consider treatment for the nicotine withdrawal. - Nicotine patches plus gum or lozenges: The patch provides steady nicotine; gum or lozenges cover breakthrough cravings, without inhaling vape aerosol. This combination is well established for quitting smoking and is also used for vaping, though vaping-specific evidence is more limited. - Varenicline: A prescription medication that reduces cravings and nicotine's rewarding effects. Clinical trials show it can help people quit vaping. In the U.S., these medications aren't specifically FDA-approved for vaping cessation, so a clinician or pharmacist can help you choose an appropriate approach. "One disposable every 4-5 days" isn't enough to choose a dose, devices vary enormously in nicotine content. 3. Plan for stress, not just nicotine. The calming effect you described is real, but some of it may be relief from withdrawal between hits, not necessarily relief from the underlying stress. Keep a quick replacement routine ready: leave your desk, take a few slow breaths, drink water, chew gum, or walk for five minutes. These don't erase finals stress, but they can help you ride out a craving. 4. Expect a rough patch, not a permanent one. Withdrawal commonly peaks in the first few days and eases over the following weeks. Medication can soften it. If you take a hit, that doesn't undo your progress; resume the plan rather than treating it as failure. In the U.S., 1-800-QUIT-NOW offers free coaching and can connect you with local resources. To make this specific: what device/nicotine strength do you use, and how soon after waking do you usually vape?

Willpower alone usually isn't enough, and that's not a character flaw. Nicotine is genuinely addictive, so the approaches with the best track record stack a few things together instead of relying on grit. What tends to work - Nicotine replacement. Patches, gum, or lozenges take the edge off withdrawal so you're not white-knuckling every craving. They're sold over the counter. A pharmacist can help you match a dose to how much you've been using. Disposables are often high-nicotine, so going from that to nothing overnight is a rough jump. - A quit plan, not a vibe. Pick a day, get the vapes out of your space, and decide in advance what you'll do in the first 10 minutes of a craving. Most cravings peak and fade in a few minutes if you don't feed them. - Outside support. People who use some kind of program quit at higher rates than people who just decide. In the US, 1-800-QUIT-NOW is free, and smokefree.gov has vaping-specific tools. Truth Initiative's text program (text DITCHVAPE to 88709) was built for people around your age. - A doctor if the basics aren't enough. Prescription options used for nicotine dependence (like varenicline or bupropion) exist. That's a conversation with a clinician, not something to self-prescribe. What the first stretch actually feels like Expect irritability, restlessness, trouble focusing, and strong cravings for several days, often peaking in the first week and easing over a few weeks. The stress relief you felt during finals was partly the nicotine taking away the discomfort it created. Early on, nothing will feel as instantly calming. That gap is temporary, but it's real, so having a replacement ready matters: a short walk, gum, a drink of water, texting someone, leaving the room. It won't match the hit at first. The point is to outlast the craving, not to feel great. Slips happen. One hit doesn't erase the attempt unless you treat it as proof you can't quit and go back to daily use. That is a huge shift. Deciding you actually want to do it for yourself (and to set a boundary for your brother) is the absolute best starting point. And you are 100% right: relying solely on "willpower" is a terrible strategy for a chemical addiction. Vaping is actually two different habits tangled together, the chemical addiction to nicotine, and the physical/behavioral habit of putting something to your mouth and inhaling. You have to hack both. Here is what actually works for people in the real world: 1. Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT) Disposables pack a massive amount of nicotine. Quitting cold turkey can make you miserable and irritable. Using nicotine patches, gum, or lozenges gives your brain the chemical it's screaming for, but breaks the hand-to-mouth vaping habit. A patch gives you a steady baseline of nicotine so you don't get the huge spikes and crashes, and you can slowly lower the dose over time. 2. Hack the physical habit (oral fixation) Your brain is used to the physical action of vaping, especially when stressed. You need a stand-in. Flavored toothpicks (cinnamon or tea tree) are great because they give a slight "bite" in the back of your throat. Mints, sunflower seeds, regular gum, or even drinking ice water through a straw with a bite-valve can help satisfy that physical twitch. 3. Change the environment Willpower fails when the vape is sitting right next to your phone. When you're ready to actually stop, you have to throw the devices away. Not in your room's trash can where you can dig it out, but in a public dumpster. 4. Address the stress piece You mentioned before that vaping was the only thing that kept you calm during finals. The secret about vaping is that a big part of the "calm" is just the fact that you are taking a slow, deep breath. You'll need to find a new way to decompress when the stress hits, whether that's stepping outside, doing a quick physical reset (like a short walk), or gaming for a few minutes. 5. Text support (seriously) There's a free text program called "This is Quitting" (from the Truth Initiative) specifically designed for young adults trying to quit vaping. It sends you texts based on your quit date and helps talk you off the ledge when cravings hit. Since you go through a disposable every 4-5 days, your body is very used to having nicotine around.
